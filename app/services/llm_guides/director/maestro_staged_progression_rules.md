STAGED PROGRESSION — A PROCESS IS TOLD ACROSS SHOTS, NOT INSIDE ONE:

- A process with stages (a plant sprouting, blooming and drying; a storm arriving and
  clearing; rain starting and stopping; someone changing clothes; light falling) is ONE
  story beat spread over SEVERAL consecutive shots. Give each shot ONE stage: the seed,
  the sprout, the bloom, the withering. Never write the whole process into a single shot,
  and never repeat an already completed process in a later shot.
- Fit the stage to the shot's own duration. Each shot has the length listed for it: a
  three-second shot holds one small visible change, not three. Compressing a multi-stage
  effect into a short shot is what makes effects flash past and lose their intention.
- To slow a process down so its detail reads, give its stages to consecutive shots instead
  of describing the same process faster.
- A later shot may name the stage it inherits from the shot before it ("the creeper that
  has just sprouted", "the flowers now browning at the edges"). This is the one place where
  a continuity reference is required rather than forbidden: a staged process is unreadable
  if every shot restates the whole thing instead of its own stage. The ban on continuity
  wording still applies to everything else, and no shot may re-perform action that has
  already happened.
- Self-contained means a shot carries everything needed to render on its own: setting,
  wardrobe, identity, composition, camera, lighting, ambience. It does NOT mean the shot
  restates the whole story. The film's events belong to the shot list as a progression,
  not copied into every shot.
- Weather, atmosphere and environment are a state to carry, not an event to re-create in
  each shot. State them once as continuity ("rain still falling across the warehouse") and
  reserve their changes -- it starts, it stops, lightning strikes -- for the shots where
  they actually change.
